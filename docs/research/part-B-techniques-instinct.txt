====================================================
1. TERM DETECTION: picking the spans worth defining
====================================================

Three families of method, and one framing problem first.

Framing problem (my inference, important): keyphrase extraction research optimizes for topic salience - the phrases that summarize a document. Your feature needs a different objective: unfamiliarity to this particular reader. A salient term the reader already knows ("closure") wastes a link; a non-salient but unfamiliar term ("zipf scale") is exactly what you want. No standard benchmark captures this, so extraction quality has to be evaluated against your own labeled examples, not published F1.

a) Classical unsupervised extractors (YAKE, RAKE, TextRank, C-value)
- YAKE scores candidates on statistical features (casing, position, frequency, relatedness to context); TextRank is graph-based (Mihalcea and Tarau 2004); RAKE splits on stopwords. All are free, instant, dependency-light. Verified via the survey literature [2, 3].
- C-value/NC-value (Frantzi and Ananiadou) targets multi-word domain terms specifically, using frequency nested inside longer terms plus context words - closer to "technical term" than to "keyphrase" [17, search-level].
- Reality check: Gallina et al.'s large-scale re-evaluation (9 datasets, JCDL 2020) found state-of-the-art models are still challenged by simple baselines on some datasets, and that benchmark setups differ enough that published comparisons are shaky [3].
- Weakness for your use: they rank by salience, not difficulty, and output bare spans with no type or reader model.

b) NER for code/technical entities
- StackOverflowNER and CodeDocsNER exist as datasets, and fine-tuned token classifiers work, but classical NER needs training per entity type [20, search-level].
- GLiNER is the practical pick in this family: a small bidirectional-transformer NER that takes arbitrary label strings at inference ("programming language concept", "algorithm", "library name") zero-shot, runs on CPU, has ONNX export and quantization paths, Apache-2.0 [11]. It also advertises streaming/incremental NER, which matters for chat UIs.

c) LLM prompting with structured JSON output
- Verified benchmark: Kang and Shin (COLING 2025) tested zero-shot keyphrase extraction with LLMs across six benchmarks. Llama3-8B-Instruct with a plain "extract keyphrases" prompt already beat the strongest unsupervised method (PromptRank) by an average of 9.43% F1@5; hybrid and role prompting pushed further; GPT-4o, Gemma2 and Qwen2 were also compared [1]. Prompts are published (github.com/kangnlp/Zero-shot-KPE-with-LLMs).
- Caveat from Song et al. 2024: prompt wording matters at the level of individual keywords, and complex prompts are not reliably better than simple ones except on long documents [2]. Budget time for prompt iteration, not prompt architecture.
- Practical advantage over (a)/(b): you can ask for exactly your schema - term, verbatim span, standard-vs-coined guess, reader-level guess, definitions - in one call, and constrained decoding can guarantee the JSON parses (see section 4).

Reader calibration. No established method found; this is inference on my part. Practical options: (i) a coarse self-declared level in the prompt ("intermediate JavaScript developer"), (ii) frequency-based proxy - terms with low Zipf frequency in a general corpus [16] are more likely unfamiliar, (iii) over time, use which tooltips the reader actually opens as the calibration signal. The hypertext literature supports caring about this: prior knowledge strongly mediates how much extra links help or hurt [5].

Link density. Verified evidence says keep it low:
- DeStefano and LeFevre's review (Computers in Human Behavior, 2007) found that the decision-making and visual-processing demands of hypertext impaired reading performance, with low-working-memory and low-prior-knowledge readers most disadvantaged; visible link typing and restricted linking helped [5].
- Wikipedia's own numbers: blue links drive ~28% of Wikipedia traffic and ~2 million hovers per minute, and their Page Previews work added a ~650 ms hover delay purely to cut accidental/unwanted previews [13].
Recommendation: rank candidate terms and cap links per answer (top 5-8), with a visible style that distinguishes "standard term, definition verified" from "coined here".

====================================================
2. STANDARD vs COINED / MODEL-SPECIFIC TERMS
====================================================

Verified-applicable signals, cheapest first:

a) Dictionary/encyclopedia existence. MediaWiki's Opensearch and Search APIs give free existence + fuzzy match checks against Wikipedia, and Wiktionary exposes the same API family [14]. A term with a direct Wikipedia/Wiktionary article is almost certainly standard. Misses are weak evidence (specialist terms often lack articles), so absence must be a soft signal, not a verdict.

b) Corpus frequency. The wordfreq library gives Zipf-scale frequencies from many combined corpora [16]; a multi-word term or compound with near-zero frequency is a coined-term candidate. The classic termhood literature (C-value/NC-value [17]) measures "is a real term in this domain corpus", which helps if you feed it a domain corpus (docs, your codebase).

c) Domain docs. MDN's full content is public Markdown on GitHub (14,000+ pages) [15] - a grep-able ground-truth lexicon for web terms. Same trick works for any docs corpus you index.

d) Search hit counts. Works but rate-limited and unversioned; fine as a tiebreaker.

e) Asking the model where the term came from. Do not trust this as ground truth. Turpin et al. (NeurIPS 2023) showed chain-of-thought explanations can systematically misrepresent the model's actual reasons, confidently rationalizing when biased [12]. A model's claim "I coined this just now" or "this is standard jargon" is a hint for triage, not evidence.

f) Tracing first use. Two different things:
- Tracing to first use in the visible conversation: fully reliable, just do it deterministically - normalized substring search over prior turns (case/punctuation-insensitive, longest match first). No model needed.
- Tracing into the model's hidden reasoning: not possible through public chat APIs, which do not expose raw chain-of-thought, and per [12] even exposed reasoning would not be a faithful ledger of where a term originated. Treat this thread of the idea as closed; the honest UI version is "first appeared in this conversation at turn N".

A working classifier for the spike: standard if (Wikipedia/Wiktionary hit OR decent corpus frequency OR present in indexed domain docs); coined-suspect if all three miss AND the conversation contains an earlier definitional use; uncertain otherwise. Label, don't hide, the uncertain class.

====================================================
3. WRITING THE DEFINITIONS
====================================================

Grounded beats ungrounded, and there is a researched reason:
- Definition modeling - generating dictionary definitions from a word embedding - is a studied task (Noraset et al. 2017; Gadetsky et al., ACL 2018) and the consistent finding is that quality collapses for rare and unseen words [18, search-level]. That is exactly your coined-term case: an LLM asked to define a term it invented will happily synthesize a confident, unsourced gloss. So: never generate dictionary-style definitions for terms the model itself coined. For those, quote the first-use context from the conversation and mark the gloss "inferred from context, not a standard term".
- Grounded pipeline for standard terms: retrieve the Wikipedia summary (REST/Opensearch [14]), the MDN page [15], or the relevant code/docs snippet; then have the model compress the retrieved text into the two output lengths with a source link. Retrieval-grounding the definition is what keeps it accurate; the model's job is compression, not recall.

Format. Two tiers, matching your tooltip/side-pane design:
- Tooltip: NN/g's guidance is that tooltips are brief, self-sufficient microcontent, must not carry info that is vital to the task, need keyboard as well as mouse hover, and should never restate the obvious [7]. Practically: one sentence, roughly 15-25 words.
- Side pane: the fuller explanation - a short paragraph, an example, the source link, and for coined terms the first-use quote and turn reference. NN/g's "not vital info only in a tooltip" rule also argues the pane link should be visible without hovering [7].
Wikipedia's Page Previews are the at-scale existence proof for this exact two-tier pattern (hover card + full article on click) [13].

====================================================
4. WHERE THE LOGIC RUNS
====================================================

Compared on latency, cost, accuracy, streaming behavior, failure modes.

(a) Post-turn second pass returning JSON
- Latency: one extra call after the answer completes; the reader sees the plain answer immediately, annotations fade in. Best perceived-latency profile.
- Cost: a second input (the answer) plus JSON output; with a small model this is cheap.
- Accuracy: good, and schema adherence is solvable - OpenAI's Structured Outputs hit 100% schema match on their complex-schema evals where a 2023 model scored under 40% [9], and llama.cpp's GBNF grammars force valid JSON locally [4]. Still keep a validate-and-retry loop: a 2026 study of 3,997 prompt-enforced generations across nine OpenAI/Anthropic models found similar validity rates hide different failure profiles, markdown-fenced JSON was the most common failure, and only 48% of fenced failures validated after fence removal - so validate, append the error, retry once [10].
- Streaming: compatible - annotate when the turn (or a paragraph) completes.
- Main failure mode (inference): span offsets drift. Mitigation: have the model return the verbatim substring for each term and re-locate it client-side with string search; never trust character offsets.

(b) Main model marks terms inline ([[term|definition]])
- Pros: one call, definitions co-generated in the same context, streams with the text.
- Cons (mostly inference, consistent with the sources): markup tokens add cost to every turn; the model can leak or half-close markers, especially mid-stream, so you need a lenient incremental parser and a raw-text fallback; constrained decoding cannot practically enforce markup embedded inside free prose (GBNF-style grammars constrain the whole output format [4]); definitions inline in the prompt contract are harder to regenerate or edit later; and it couples annotation quality to the main model's attention on its primary task.
- Verdict: acceptable for a prototype demo, fragile in production.

(c) Small / local model
- GLiNER for spans: zero-shot custom labels, CPU-friendly, ONNX, Apache-2.0, even streaming NER [11]. A small instruct model behind llama.cpp GBNF can emit the JSON [4].
- Pros: zero marginal cost, low latency, private, works offline.
- Cons: weaker long-tail judgment, no reader model, and the definition quality for unusual terms drops - pair it with retrieval (section 3) rather than free generation.
- Verdict: the right eventual home for the extraction half; probably not for definition writing.

Recommended shape: (a) now, (c) for extraction + retrieval for definitions later. Hybrid is also plausible: local GLiNER proposes spans, one cloud call does classification + definitions.

====================================================
5. EVALUATION: DOES IT ACTUALLY HELP
====================================================

The most transferable research base is second-language "glossing" (marginal/popup definitions while reading) and hypertext reading:

- Glossing works. A 2026 meta-analysis (26 studies, 123 samples) found a large overall effect of glosses on incidental vocabulary learning (Hedges' d = 0.935, 95% CI [0.780, 1.089]), robust in sensitivity analysis - but with very high heterogeneity (I-squared = 89%), so design details matter [6]. Subgroup patterns: interactional/clickable glosses d = 1.494 vs plain positional/marginal glosses d = 0.375; more exposures and longer treatment increased effects [6]. This is direct evidence for your tooltip+click design over static marginal notes.
- But links have a cost. DeStefano and LeFevre: hypertext's decision and visual-processing demands impaired comprehension, worst for low-prior-knowledge readers; restricted linking and visible link types mitigated [5]. So evaluate density conditions, not just on/off.
- Comprehension measures that are cheap to run: the cloze procedure (Taylor 1953 - delete every nth word, score correct fills) tracks with Flesch and Dale-Chall readability and measures actual comprehension rather than surface features [8]; glossing studies typically add meaning-recognition and meaning-recall vocab tests, immediate and delayed [6].
- Process metrics: tooltip-open rate per term (which doubles as the calibration signal for section 1), time-to-finish, backtracking.
- Design: within-subject, counterbalanced - same reader, alternating annotated vs plain answers on matched topics; immediate comprehension quiz (cloze + a few meaning-recognition items) plus a delayed retention check a day later; report effects separately by reader level, because both [5] and [6] say level moderates everything.
- Industry precedent for the UI: Wikimedia A/B-tested Page Previews at full Wikipedia scale before shipping [13] - their published notes are a useful template for measuring a reading-aid feature honestly.

====================================================
6. RECOMMENDED PIPELINE, ONE-DAY SPIKE (Hermes Desktop plugin, plain JS)
====================================================

1. Trigger after a turn completes. Send the answer text to one model call with a published-style extraction prompt [1] adapted to: "list up to 8 terms an intermediate developer likely cannot define; for each: verbatim span, kind (standard/coined/uncertain), tooltip definition (max 25 words), pane definition (max 120 words)".
2. Enforce the schema (Structured Outputs or equivalent) [9]; validate and retry once with the error appended on failure [10].
3. Client-side: locate each span by verbatim string search; skip terms not found. Never use model-supplied offsets.
4. Tooltip on hover with a ~650 ms delay [13], keyboard-focusable [7], click opens the side pane. Cap at 8 terms per answer [5].
5. Coined/uncertain terms get a distinct style and the pane shows the first-use quote ("first appeared in turn 3") with the gloss labeled "inferred from context" - no invented dictionary definitions [18][12].
6. Acceptance check: run it over ~10 saved dense answers, hand-label precision/recall of term choice and correctness of each definition.

What the spike deliberately skips: calibration, grounding, density learning - all need usage data the spike will start producing.

====================================================
7. BETTER PIPELINE, LATER
====================================================

1. Local GLiNER proposes candidate spans with custom labels, streaming as the answer renders [11].
2. Termhood/classification filter: Wikipedia/Wiktionary existence [14], corpus frequency [16], domain docs index (MDN etc.) [15] -> standard / coined / uncertain.
3. Grounded definitions: retrieve (Wikipedia summary, MDN page, codebase snippet), then LLM compresses to the two tiers with a source link; LLM-only generation only as a labeled fallback; coined terms always quote first use, never a fabricated gloss.
4. Per-reader calibration from tooltip-open history: stop linking terms the reader never opens, lower the threshold where they always do.
5. Density policy from measurement: start at top-5, A/B against top-10, decide by comprehension and annoyance metrics (section 5), not taste.
6. Standing eval harness: cloze + meaning-recall quizzes, immediate and delayed, stratified by reader level; definition cache per term so cost and latency drop over time.
